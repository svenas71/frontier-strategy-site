A few years ago I was standing outside the beautiful, newly built Francis Crick Auditorium on the Wellcome Genome Campus just outside Cambridge. I was attending a conference on advances in systems biology. The campus is immaculate, a blend of contemporary architecture and the rather imposing Georgian grandeur of Hinxton Hall, the 18th-century manor house situated just opposite the Crick Auditorium.

At one of the coffee breaks, I started to have a conversation with one of the speakers who had just presented the crystal structure of a human histone methyltransferase. Spectacular work. A fantastic crystal structure, of course, in immaculate atomic detail.

I congratulated him and said what a great achievement it was, and mentioned that I was also working on histone modifications and epigenetics.

His demeanour struck me as odd because he was pretty downbeat and didn’t seem particularly enthusiastic. I looked at him, and his words have been in my mind since that day.

Because what he said was:

“Yeah, well, it looks just like that from yeast.”

He was depressed or downtrodden because his DNA histone methyltransferase, at the atomic level, looked nearly exactly like the one from yeast! A single-celled organism, one of the simplest eukaryotes imaginable, separated from the human lineage by hundreds of millions of years of evolution. Yet, at the atomic level, the methyltransferase looked nearly exactly the same.

For me, this was a spectacular result and evidence of the incredible conservation in biology over this inordinately long time.

Fast forward to today, and we are looking at oncology from a completely different angle, measuring circulating cell-free DNA to determine cancer burden.

Time to diagnosis is critical. The development of disease progression is critical. Several people that I’ve spoken to from the pharmaceutical industry or CROs have again and again mentioned to me how important identifying non-responders, for example, to a cancer treatment would be. Nearly pleading with me to look into that.

We want to.

In the company that I run, we are now at a position where we can have the following value proposition:

What if we can not only gather fundamentally important data at scale, but what if we could also compress the timelines of being able to gather that data?

In comes the helping hand of evolution and evolutionary conservation.

A regular clinical trial takes upwards of one and a half to two years, not counting the time and the money spent preparing the clinical trial itself. The costs of these are, as is known in the industry, high, if not to say astronomically high, with an average cost per patient of upwards of €10,000.

Trials take years to design, years to run, and sometimes years to analyse.

And time, as we all know, is the one irreversible resource that is one of the most scarce in our current world.

What if we could compress that timeline?

Here’s what we’re thinking. And here’s where evolution is lending a helping hand, as well as a providing us with a massive perk.

Now let us think about what we’re actually doing. We’re measuring circulating cell-free DNA as an indication of tumour burden or tumour incidence in dogs.

Dogs are wonderful companion animals. All of us love dogs. They are cherished members of the family. They take care of us just as much as we take care of them.

Yet they get sick. And unlike humans, dogs find it very hard to communicate with us about their inner state, and rarely do. Which, as a result, means that severe disease is nearly always diagnosed far too late for an early intervention that could be less impactful or less detrimental to their health.

By the time humans or veterinarians notice a severe disease, for example cancer, the disease has often already progressed to a fairly late stage.

That is why early cancer diagnosis is so vitally important. And this is the focus of testblu.

Now, we also all know that dogs have a much shorter lifespan than we do. And, interestingly, they also have a faster rate of disease progression in cancer.

As dire as this may be for our beloved animals, it harbours an important opportunity. If disease progression is actually quicker, this means the timelines for any interventional or observational study are also shorter.

We should also be aware that dogs, overall, are *far* more closely related to us than the yeast in my story, which already shows a remarkable resemblance to us on a molecular scale.

And while I would not extrapolate the conservation of one of the most conserved biological processes, namely histone modification and epigenetics, to all other processes in an organism, dogs *do* share a huge amount of metabolic and organismal similarity, or even identity, with us humans.

This is for many reasons. Firstly, they are mammals, just like us. But additionally, they have co-evolved in our environment for more than 20,000 years. They have very similar diets to us. That means they suffer similar environmental burdens contributing to disease progression. 

Many of the major factors that contribute to cancer, to spontaneous cancer generation, dogs and humans share, on top of their shared biology.

This is not news.

And dogs are routinely, if on a small scale, used as model organisms for cancer research.

There is also a significant amount of work on cancer diagnosis and even cancer treatment through chemotherapy and other therapeutic approaches in dogs.

Going forward, being able to generate *high-quality diagnostic data* across a very important indication such as cancer is, of course, also of high value to humans.

But imagine now compressing those timelines by a factor of two or three and gain significant high-value information at the same time.

This is what we’re proposing to do at testblu.

And this is our unique opportunity to save not just on money, but on the other resource that we have the least amount of control over: which is Time.

Evolution has already done enormous amounts of work for us.

Let’s put that to good use.

### Supporting literature

1. **Fenger & London (2026).** Dogs as a Model for Cancer: An Update. *Annual Review of Animal Biosciences*, 14:341–374. [DOI](https://doi.org/10.1146/annurev-animal-111523-101930)

   The authors compare treatment assessment timescales of 1–2 years in dogs versus 5–10 years in humans. They also discuss molecular similarities, therapeutic resistance and the development of drug-resistant metastases.

2. **Regan, Garcia & Thamm (2019).** A Comparative Approach to Accelerate Translational Drug Development. *ILAR Journal*, 59(1):99–110. [DOI](https://doi.org/10.1093/ilar/ily019)

   The shorter lifespan of dogs allows for more rapid attainment of study endpoints and efficacy data.

3. **LeBlanc et al. (2016).** Defining the Value of a Comparative Approach to Cancer Drug Development. *Clinical Cancer Research*. [DOI](https://doi.org/10.1158/1078-0432.CCR-15-2347)

   The authors emphasise the shorter timelines and lower costs of comparative oncology studies, as well as their potential to directly inform subsequent human clinical trial design.

4. **Bringing the Genomic Revolution to Comparative Oncology: Human and Dog Cancers (2024).** *Annual Review of Biomedical Data Science*, 7:107–129.

   A review of the genomic foundations of comparative oncology and the opportunities arising from studying naturally occurring cancers across dogs and humans.
